Calm

Calm is a cardiac intervention.

Stress isn't just unpleasant — it raises blood pressure, stirs inflammation, and wears on the heart. The good news: the antidote is free, portable, and starts working within a few breaths.

TRY IT NOWOne round of box breathing

Box breathing — inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4 — helps regulate the nervous system and can lower blood pressure. Follow the circle.

Ready

Press start, then breathe with the circle.

TECHNIQUETwo breaths worth learning

▢

Box breathing (4-4-4-4)

Inhale 4s, hold 4s, exhale 4s, hold 4s. Steadies the autonomic nervous system and can ease blood pressure. Great for focus and acute stress.

◖

4-7-8 breathing

Inhale 4s, hold 7s, exhale 8s. The long exhale is especially good for winding down anxiety and easing into sleep.

Practised regularly, these improve heart-rate variability, lower cortisol, and sharpen day-to-day stress management — and they stack neatly on top of a heart-healthy diet and movement.

MEDITATIONWhy stillness reaches the heart

Raj yoga and mindfulness meditation both shift the nervous system from "fight or flight" toward "rest and repair." That single change ripples outward:

  • Lower blood pressure — up to a 14 mmHg systolic drop reported in cardiac patients practising mindfulness
  • Slower, steadier heart rate and better heart-rate variability
  • Reduced anxiety and lower cortisol — both heart-disease risk factors
  • Better sleep, which quietly protects the cardiovascular system

You don't need rituals or special equipment — just a few quiet minutes a day, returning your attention to the breath or a calm, positive thought.

OUTLOOKOptimism is protective

Positive emotional well-being tracks with real cardiovascular benefit: people higher in optimism and "emotional vitality" have shown a 20–30% lower risk of coronary heart disease over time — via lower blood pressure, less inflammation, and healthier habits overall.

Gratitude, mindfulness, and staying connected to other people are heart medicine too.

A gentle note: breathing and meditation are wonderful complements to care for anxiety, low mood, or chronic stress — but they're complements, not replacements. If you're struggling, please reach out to a GP, counsellor, or someone you trust. Support helps, and asking for it is a strength.

Sources referenced: PMC and journal studies on Raj yoga and mindfulness meditation in cardiovascular care; Harvard Health on meditation and heart benefits; NHS, Healthline and Medical News Today on breathing techniques; PMC reviews on emotional well-being and coronary risk; American Heart Association and World Heart Federation on mental well-being.

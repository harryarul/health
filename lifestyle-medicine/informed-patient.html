Partner

Be a partner in your care.

Medicines save and improve countless lives. They also work best when you understand them — what they're for, how to take them, and what to watch for. Informed patients get safer, better care. This page is about asking good questions, not avoiding treatment.

!

Read this first. Nothing here is a reason to stop or skip a prescribed medicine. Stopping suddenly can be more dangerous than the medicine itself.

If something worries you, the move is always the same: talk to your doctor or pharmacist and decide together. This page helps you have that conversation well.

STEP 1Keep one current medication list

The single most useful habit in all of medication safety. Keep an up-to-date list of everything you take — prescriptions, over-the-counter products, vitamins and supplements — and show it at every appointment and pharmacy visit.

  • Name, dose, and what each one is for
  • Who prescribed it and when it started
  • Over-the-counter medicines and supplements (these interact too)
  • Any allergies or past bad reactions

This is your best defence when you see more than one prescriber — it's how conflicting prescriptions get caught.

STEP 2Ask five questions before you start

1

What is this for, and what should improve?

2

How and when do I take it — and for how long?

3

What side effects should I watch for, and which need urgent attention?

4

Does it interact with my other medicines, food, or alcohol?

5

Are there lifestyle changes or alternatives we could try alongside it?

Good clinicians welcome these questions. Asking for a second opinion when you're unsure is your right, not a slight.

STEP 3Know the common safety themes

You don't need a pharmacology degree — just awareness of where risk concentrates.

⚠

Adverse reactions

Unwanted effects can happen even at normal doses. Read the leaflet, and remember a new symptom may be the medicine rather than a new illness. Report it.

⇄

Interactions

Two medicines can change each other's effect. Your pharmacist can screen for this — which is exactly why one current list matters so much.

∑

Polypharmacy

Taking five or more medicines raises interaction and side-effect risk, especially for older adults. Ask for a periodic "medication review."

◷

Long-term use

Some medicines need monitoring over time. Regular reviews keep the benefit-vs-risk balance honest as your health changes.

STEP 4Report side effects — early

Reporting isn't just for you; it improves drug safety for everyone. Tell your doctor or pharmacist about unusual symptoms promptly. In the UK you can also report directly through the MHRA Yellow Card scheme; many countries have an equivalent.

One specific example worth knowing: when starting or changing certain medicines — including some used for mood — a few people develop akathisia, an intense inner restlessness and inability to sit still. It can be mistaken for worsening anxiety. If you or someone you care for feels this after a medication change, contact the prescriber promptly so it can be managed — but don't stop the medicine on your own, as abrupt withdrawal carries its own risks.

On mental-health medicines specifically: antidepressants and similar treatments help a great many people, and stopping them suddenly can be harmful. If you have concerns about a mood medicine, raise them with your prescriber — any change should be planned and supported, never abrupt. If you're in distress or having thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out to a doctor, a crisis line, or someone you trust right away. You deserve support, and it's available.

STEP 5Use the system's safeguards

Patient advocates have helped strengthen the safeguards around medicines — clearer warnings, better post-approval monitoring, more transparency. You benefit from that history every time you ask a question, read a leaflet, or request a review. Engaged patients make the whole system safer.

The goal isn't fewer medicines or more medicines. It's the right ones, understood, and revisited as you change.

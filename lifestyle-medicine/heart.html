The flagship

Your heart can heal.

Coronary artery disease has long been treated as a one-way street. A growing body of research says otherwise: with the right lifestyle, narrowed arteries can actually open back up.

THE STUDYThe Mount Abu Open Heart Trial

Patients with documented coronary artery disease followed a structured "healthy and happy lifestyle" programme: a low-fat vegetarian diet, moderate exercise, and stress management through Raj yoga meditation. Those who adhered most closely showed significant regression of coronary lesions — the plaque narrowing their arteries shrank — and experienced fewer cardiac events than those who didn't.

The intervention wasn't one heroic change. It was four ordinary habits, practised together, every day.

PILLAR 1Eat mostly plants

A well-planned plant-forward diet hits cardiovascular risk from several directions at once.

↓

Lower cholesterol

A meta-analysis of 30 randomised trials found vegetarian and vegan diets cut total cholesterol by about 7% and LDL by about 10% — roughly a third of a statin's effect, from food alone.

♥

Better risk markers

Plant-based patterns have lowered LDL, improved blood-sugar control (HbA1c), reduced body weight, and improved triglyceride profiles in people with coronary disease.

✷

Antioxidants from the plate

Vitamin C and E, selenium, and lycopene help limit the oxidative stress that damages arteries — best obtained from whole foods, not supplements, where trials are mixed.

You don't have to become strictly vegetarian. Shifting the balance toward vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts and fruit — while cutting saturated and trans fats — moves the needle. Go deeper in Food as Medicine →

PILLAR 2Move, moderately

The trial used moderate exercise, not punishing workouts. Regular, sustainable movement improves how the heart and blood vessels function and supports every other change you make. Think brisk daily walks you'll actually keep doing — consistency beats intensity for the heart.

PILLAR 3Quiet the stress response

Raj yoga meditation — practised without rituals or mantras, simply focusing on calm, positive thought — was the programme's stress-management engine, and it has measurable cardiovascular effects.

  • Shifts the nervous system from "fight or flight" toward "rest and repair" (parasympathetic tone)
  • Helps stabilise blood pressure and lower cortisol, a stress hormone
  • Reduced pre-operative anxiety in patients facing major cardiac surgery
  • Improves sleep quality — itself a quiet pillar of heart health

Mindfulness meditation shows similar promise: one meta-analysis reported a 14 mmHg fall in systolic blood pressure in cardiac patients, alongside steadier heart rate and improved walking distance. Try the breathing tool in Mind & Breath →

PILLAR 4Tend your emotional life

The "happy" half of the programme isn't decoration. People with higher optimism and emotional vitality have shown a 20–30% lower risk of developing coronary heart disease over time — through lower blood pressure, less inflammation, healthier lipids, and a greater tendency to keep up the other healthy habits.

Gratitude practice, mindfulness, and strong social connection all feed this. A happier heart is, quite literally, a healthier one.

THE MINDSETBe the lead in your own care

In heart-failure patients, an empowerment-based self-care programme — personal goals, symptom monitoring, real skills — cut emergency visits by about 55% and hospital admissions by about 62% compared with standard education. Knowing how to care for yourself, day to day, changes outcomes.

!

Lifestyle works best as a partner to your medical care, not a replacement.

If you have heart disease or take medication for blood pressure, cholesterol, or your heart, bring this programme to your doctor. Many will help you adopt it and adjust medication safely as your numbers improve. Never stop a prescribed heart medicine on your own — that can be dangerous.

Sources referenced: Mount Abu Open Heart Trial; JAMA Network Open and PMC analyses of plant-based diets and cardiovascular risk; Harvard Health and NHS dietary guidance; PMC studies on Raj yoga and mindfulness meditation in cardiac care; world-heart-federation.org and the American Heart Association on emotional well-being.
